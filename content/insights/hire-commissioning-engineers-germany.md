## Is there really no commissioning talent in the German market?

Usually there is. Most stalled searches in Germany fail on two things the employer controls, the salary band and the brief underneath the job title, and not on a genuine shortage. Fix those two and the shortlist that looked impossible starts to fill. The eight questions below tell you which one is costing you.

I ask every prospective client these before I agree to a conversation. They screen hard, and that's the point. Run them on yourself first and you'll either fix the search without me, or you'll know exactly why you need a specialist. Either way you come out ahead.

## How many hires are you planning, and where?

Start with scale and geography, because they change everything that follows. One commissioning engineer for a single Frankfurt build is a different search from ten across three German cities over a year. The market for mission-critical MEP talent in Germany is deep in some roles and paper-thin in others, and the city matters as much as the count.

If your roles sit outside Germany, most of what follows still holds, but the bands and the candidate pools won't. This piece is written for the German data centre and MEP market, where I work and where the competition for the right people is fiercest.

## Which roles keep breaking, and what actually broke?

This is the question that matters most, and the one people answer with the polished version. Name the exact role, the city, and what broke down. "Commissioning engineers in Frankfurt, the agency shortlist never matched the technical brief" is useful. "Hiring is hard right now" is not.

In my experience two failures cause most stalled searches, and both are fixable.

### The salary band was wrong

Most "impossible" roles are priced below the market, or priced right but benchmarked against the wrong role. When a delivery lead tells me commissioning talent in Frankfurt can't be found, I already know the band, because I've placed into it. A Senior Authorised Person, the person who controls access to live electrical systems and issues permits to work, went into a Frankfurt data centre role at €90,000. An Electrical Construction Manager in the same city went in at €65,000. If your offer sits well under those, the shortage you're seeing is a pricing problem in disguise.

One more thing worth weighing on that Senior Authorised Person. The €90,000 is the permanent figure. The same person on a freelance or contract basis can command close to double, and on a live build under time pressure some firms decide the day rate is worth every cent. Permanent and contract are two different markets for the same skill set, and pricing a permanent role as if the contract market didn't exist leaves you stuck.

### The brief underneath the title didn't match

Two people can hold the same job title and be worth €10,000 apart, because the work behind the title is different. I placed two Senior Mechanical Engineers in Frankfurt, same title on paper, €10,000 apart in salary, because one brief demanded design depth and project-phase experience the other didn't. A generalist agency reads the title and sends three CVs against it. The CVs look wrong to you, the seat stays empty, and everyone blames the market. The market was never the problem.

## How are you hiring right now?

List every channel honestly. Internal team, preferred-supplier agencies, ad hoc briefings, job boards, executive search for the senior seats, or building the function for the first time. Each one has a blind spot, and the biggest is shared across all of them.

The people you actually want are employed, heads-down on someone else's project, and they never see a job advert. Job boards and direct adverts reach the people who are looking. The engineer who'd change your build isn't among them. Reaching that person is a different job from posting a vacancy, and it's the job most hiring setups quietly skip.

## What would success actually look like in twelve months?

Be specific, because vague goals produce vague searches. "Three commissioning engineers placed in Germany by Q3, all still in post at twelve months" is a target you can hold a partner to. "Better hiring" is a wish. The gap between those two sentences is the gap between a search that lands and one that drifts.

If you can't write the specific version, that's worth knowing on its own. It usually means the need is real but the plan behind it hasn't been made yet, and that's a conversation worth having before anyone starts sending CVs.

## Who owns the decision, and why now?

Two questions, one answer that decides everything. Know who actually signs off the hiring strategy, and make sure that person is in the room, because a search run past a decision-maker who was never consulted stalls halfway through every time.

Then the one that matters more than any other. Why now? A new project pipeline, a search that already failed, a growth phase, a new market. Something changed, or you wouldn't be reading this. Naming that change is what turns a general wish for better hiring into a search with a shape and a deadline. If nothing has changed, there's no real urgency, and no specialist can manufacture one for you.

## So is it the market, or is it you?

Run the eight and you'll usually find the answer sitting in the middle two. The band was off, or the brief didn't match the work, or both. Those are fixable, and they're fixable faster by someone who already knows the German bands because they set them.

That's the whole reason we make prospective clients answer these before a call. We only take on work we believe we can do, and the eight questions are how we find out. If your answers point at a real trigger and a search we can win, that's when the conversation is worth having.
