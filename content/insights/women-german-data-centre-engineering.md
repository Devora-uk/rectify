German data centre operators will tell you, at length, that they cannot find the engineers to build and run their sites. The commissioning managers, the electrical and mechanical specialists, the people a launch date genuinely hangs on. We set out that shortage, and why it bites harder than any megawatt figure suggests, in our piece on [why talent, not power, is the real constraint on German data centres](/insights/germany-data-centre-talent-shortage).

Here is the part that rarely makes the same conversation. About 5% of the technical staff in German data centres are women, roughly 1,300 people, and only around 10% across all roles. A sector that says it is desperate for engineers is hiring almost entirely from half the available population. Set the fairness argument aside for a moment. The sharper question for an operator losing the race to hire is what a 5% workforce actually costs.

## Why does the German data centre sector employ so few women engineers?

Because the engineering pipeline that feeds it loses women at every stage, and the sector has spent years treating the result as a fairness question rather than a hiring one. About 5% of technical data centre staff in Germany are women, roughly 1,300, with around 10% across all roles.

The loss starts long before anyone reaches a data hall. Around one in five engineering students at German universities is a woman, close to 21% of engineering, manufacturing and construction graduates. Look at people actually working as engineers in German mechanical and plant engineering, and the share falls to about 11%, lower again in senior roles. Germany also sits near the bottom of the EU league for female scientists and engineers, at roughly a third overall, with Hessen, the state that holds Frankfurt, no better than the national picture.

So the data hall sits at the bottom of a system that sheds women at every step from lecture theatre to site. Each of those steps is a place where qualified people leave a field that is short of them. Read together, the numbers describe a supply problem the sector has largely built for itself.

## How much engineering talent is actually on the table?

Enough to change the arithmetic, and you can size it. The reserve sits at every stage of that leak, and each stage the sector recovers widens the bench it hires from.

Stack the figures and the opportunity is plain. Five percent in the data hall, against roughly 11% in working engineering, against about 20% in the lecture theatre, against a third across the wider sciences. Every gap between those numbers is a pool of qualified or nearly qualified people the sector is not converting into hires.

The pattern runs well beyond Germany. Uptime Institute puts women at around 8% of the global data centre workforce, below construction at 11%, mining at 15% and manufacturing at 29%. More than three-quarters of operators report a workforce that is 10% women or less, a share unchanged since 2018, and one in five employ no women at all in their design and operations teams. A shortage this loud, sitting next to a reserve this large, is a strategy problem before it is anything else.

## What does a 5% workforce cost an operator hiring against a date?

It narrows an already thin bench to something close to half its potential size, at the exact moment you can least afford the loss. When commissioning engineers are the binding constraint, hiring from 5% of the room is a self-inflicted part of the shortage.

The German market is short of precisely the people a 5% workforce screens out by default, the experienced electrical, mechanical and controls engineers who make a site work. Operators already name competition with other data centres and rising salary demands as their main hiring obstacles. Drawing that competition from a pool you have quietly narrowed makes the poaching war harder, not easier.

Every experienced woman engineer who studied, qualified and then left the field is a candidate a rival never had to outbid you for, because she was never in the running. The cost does not arrive as a line item. It arrives as a slower fill, a higher offer, and a commissioning date that moves to the right.

## Why do women leave German engineering before they reach the data hall?

The losses cluster at two points, the move from study into work, and mid-career. A study by RWTH Aachen with the German Engineering Association found women study engineering in reasonable numbers, then either never enter the sector or leave it, often over workplace culture and whether their competence is recognised.

The reasons are well documented. New female engineers report that being accepted and judged on their work, rather than their gender, decides whether they stay. Uptime's own research described the data centre hiring environment as a short-staffed boys' club, with harassment and bias named as retention problems rather than side issues.

Add the career break many women take for childcare or family care, and a hiring system that treats any gap on a CV as a warning sign, and the leak starts to explain itself. None of this needs a cultural revolution to shift. It is a set of specific, repeated failures, and each one has a known counter that operators can apply on their own terms.

## What actually moves the number?

Methods with evidence behind them, not slogans on a careers page. The strongest is the returnship, a paid and supported route back for engineers who left the field and want to return.

The outcomes are real, though most of the hard numbers come from the US and UK, so treat them as transferable evidence rather than German proof. A long-running STEM re-entry programme run by the Society of Women Engineers put 600 returners through between 2015 and 2019, and 86% were working full time afterwards. Schneider Electric, a power and electrical infrastructure firm sitting right next to the data centre world, ran a returnship that hired five of its first seven candidates and then scaled the intake.

Alongside that, Uptime's practical advice is cheap and unglamorous. Rewrite the inflated job requirements that screen out anyone with a non-linear path, train and mentor on the job, and offer terms that suit people with family obligations. Requirement-rewriting matters most of all, because a specification demanding ten uninterrupted years of experience quietly bins the exact returners the sector needs back.

## How Rectify reads this

The reserve is real and the levers work, and neither fact turns into a hire on its own. Widening the pool only pays off if you can then vet the people it brings you, a returning commissioning engineer, a mechanical specialist who took three years out, against the specific demands of a mission-critical site. A poster campaign does not do that. Careful sourcing and technical judgement do.

Reading the whole talent market, including the half of it the sector has trained itself to overlook, is one part of the job. Moving on a specific role faster than a competitor, with the judgement to know a CV is real, is the rest of it. That is the work we do.

## What should operators hiring in Germany do about it?

Treat the 5% as a supply line, not a statistic. Three moves do most of the work: widen the specification, build or buy a returner route, and vet on competence rather than continuous service.

Start with the job description, because it is the cheapest thing to change and it does the most damage as written. A requirement list built for a perfect linear career screens out returners, career-changers and plenty of people the sector has already half-lost. Then decide whether to run your own re-entry route or partner with people who already do, and hold those roles open to candidates a standard filter would reject on a CV gap alone.

If you are planning German capacity and want to understand where the movable engineers actually sit, including the ones your competitors are not even looking at, that is a conversation worth having early.
