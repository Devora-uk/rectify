Germany already runs the largest data centre market in Europe, with 1,994 facilities and roughly 1,955 MW of live IT power as of January 2024. The build-out is not slowing. Capacity has more than doubled since 2010, and the federal economics ministry expects it to reach 4,800 MW by 2030 and pass 12,000 MW by 2045.

Almost every conversation about that growth lands on the same subject. Power. Grid access, energy prices, where the next megawatt comes from. It is a fair worry, and a real one. It is also not the thing keeping operators awake at night. Ask them directly and a different answer comes back, one that has nothing to do with electricity and everything to do with people.

## Why is talent, not power, the real constraint on German data centres?

Because operators say so. Among enterprise data centre operators in Germany, finding qualified technical staff ranks as the number-one challenge for the next three years, cited by 50%, ahead of energy prices and regulation. Outside Frankfurt, 65% call the skills shortage their single biggest problem.

Those figures come from the German Datacenter Association's Data Center Impact Report 2024, the first systematic measurement of the German market. Read them as directional rather than precise, since they rest on operator surveys, but the direction is unambiguous and it is consistent across the sector.

The logic is simple once you sit with it. Power is a capital and planning problem. Given enough money, time and grid negotiation, it gets solved. A commissioning engineer is a different kind of problem. You cannot pour concrete around the gap, and you cannot buy a person who is already working three sites over. A hall with power and cooling and no one qualified to sign it off does not go live. It sits there, burning capital, waiting.

## The roles that decide whether a site goes live

The German data centre story tends to get told as an IT and cloud story. Servers, networks, the hyperscale names on the front of the building. That framing quietly hides where the hardest hiring actually sits. The people who design, build and commission the physical plant, the electrical, mechanical and building-services engineers, are the ones a launch date genuinely hangs on.

Look at how the workforce breaks down and it stops being an abstract point.

{{workforce}}

Building technicians alone are more than a fifth of the workforce, and once you fold in the site and project management that runs mechanical and electrical delivery, the physical-plant side is roughly a third of every data centre payroll. These are the HLKS, ELT and Technischer Systemplaner roles, the Projektleiter who carries a build, the authorised person who signs off electrical works. They rarely make the press release. They are also the roles no commissioning schedule can move without.

And the way sites get built makes this structural, not seasonal. A typical German data centre employs 50 to 100 people, and colocation and hyperscale operators lean on around 1,600 external contractors alongside their 5,000 permanent staff. Contract and specialist labour is baked into how a hall gets commissioned. That demand does not switch off when a site opens, it moves straight to the next one.

## Where is the German data centre talent shortage worst?

Everywhere, not just Frankfurt. Finding qualified technical staff is a top-three challenge for 61% of Frankfurt-region colocation operators and 64% for operators elsewhere. As Frankfurt's grid tightens, new capacity is spreading to [Berlin](/areas/germany/berlin) and the Rhineland, and the hiring pressure travels with it.

[Frankfurt](/areas/germany/frankfurt) is still the centre of gravity. It carries around 745 MW of live IT load, with another 542 MW under construction and 383 MW in planning. Vacancy sits near 7%, and it added a record 134 MW in a single year. It is the deepest talent pool in the country and the tightest market to hire in, both at once.

The wider labour data says the squeeze is national. The OECD found that Germany carries 177% more vacancies per employed person in ICT roles than in the average job, rising to 255% in Saxony and sitting at 82% in Berlin. That figure covers ICT broadly rather than data centres alone, so treat it as a read on the wider pool operators fish in, not a direct headcount. As a signal of tightness across regions, it is hard to argue with.

## A pipeline that produces almost nobody

Here is the part that should worry anyone planning headcount past this year. The shortage is not mainly a poaching problem or a pay problem, though it is becoming both. At root it is a manufacturing problem, and the factory is barely running.

The biggest single obstacle to hiring is a lack of qualified young professionals, flagged by 79% of colocation operators and 57% of enterprise operators. Set that against the training numbers. Across a colocation and hyperscale workforce of roughly 5,000, the sector counted about 250 trainees and 40 dual-study students, and the report warns plainly that the training system is not built around data centre needs.

There is a large pool going almost untouched, too. Only about 5% of technical data centre staff in Germany are women, roughly 1,300 people, and just 10% across all roles. As a fairness point, that is an old conversation. As a recruitment problem, it is a talent reserve the sector has barely started to work. You cannot hire your way out of a shortage the schools and apprenticeships are not filling. Not this decade. In the meantime, the experienced engineers who already exist get fought over, hard.

## What is the shortage doing to pay and retention?

Pushing both upward. 55% of colocation operators name competition with other data centres as a hiring obstacle, and 38% point to high salary demands from experienced technical staff, with enterprise operators close behind at 35%. Poaching has become a standing cost of doing business, not an occasional shock.

When supply is fixed and demand keeps climbing, price moves. The capital behind that demand is not modest. Colocation capacity alone is projected to rise from about 1.3 GW to 3.3 GW by 2029, a build-out justifying more than €24 billion of investment over five years. The sector already contributes around €10.4 billion to German GDP, forecast to more than double to €23 billion by 2029.

All of that capex competes for the same finite bench of qualified engineers. The wider tech pool is deep on paper. Germany had 2.1 million ICT specialists in 2023, the most of any EU country. But depth on paper is not the same as availability at the desk. Across the EU, 57.5% of firms that tried to recruit ICT specialists in 2023 struggled to fill the roles, and Brussels has set a target of 20 million ICT specialists by 2030 precisely because the gap is structural. The people exist somewhere. Getting the right one, in the right discipline, to move to your site on your timeline is the actual job.

## What this means if you are hiring against a commissioning date

Strip the noise away and three things hold. The binding constraint on German data centre growth is people, not power. The scarcest of those people are the building-services and commissioning engineers who make the physical plant work, the ones the headlines skip. And the pipeline that should replace them is producing a rounding error, which means the pressure gets worse before it eases, not better.

If your plan leans on hiring these engineers at pace, the market is tighter than a raw vacancy count suggests, and it stays tight for years. Knowing the discipline, the German titles, the salary bands and where the movable people actually sit is the difference between a slipped launch and a live hall.
