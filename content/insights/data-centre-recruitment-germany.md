Rectify International is a specialist recruitment firm placing MEP, building-services engineering and data centre talent, with a primary focus on Germany and the United States.

The recruiter who charges you nothing until they place someone looks like the safe choice. On the one hire your build actually depends on, that is the most expensive decision you can make. Germany can approve sites, secure grid connections and raise capital faster than it can find the engineers to build and run them, which means the way you hire has stopped being an admin question and become a scheduling risk.

## Why data centre recruitment in Germany is harder than the build

Germany is Europe's largest data centre market, worth around $10.41 billion in 2026, and the engineering talent to build it is the part in shortest supply. [Roughly 46% of European operators cannot find qualified candidates](https://www.airswift.com/blog/data-center-recruitment-strategies) (Airswift, 2025), and industry analysis now ranks the skills shortage ahead of power and land as the binding constraint on new capacity.

The shortage is structural, not a blip. An ageing base of electrical, mechanical and controls engineers is retiring, semiconductor and energy firms chase the same people, and AI-era facilities need skills that barely existed five years ago. From July 2026, new German sites face a PUE threshold of 1.2, which loads the hardest design and commissioning work onto the most experienced engineers, the exact people already hardest to find. We have written separately on [why this shortage is a European problem, not a local one](/insights/european-data-centre-talent-shortage).

## Why the recruitment model decides whether you hit your dates

When the people are the constraint, how you go and find them is the decision that carries the programme. A senior data centre search now runs six to nine months from brief to start, so the model you pick either buys you that runway or wastes it.

Most operators still hire the way they always have, handing the role to whoever works on a no-win-no-fee basis and waiting for CVs to arrive. For a role people apply for, that works fine. For a high-voltage commissioning engineer on a live hall in Frankfurt, it quietly sets you up to miss the date.

## The false economy of "no fee until we place someone"

A contingency fee sounds like the agency is carrying your risk. What it means is that nothing is committed, so your hardest role sits in a queue behind every easier one the recruiter is working, because the easy fills pay first.

It also means they are selling you from a database. Sometimes that database is strong, built over years of placements and referrals, and the person you need is already in it. But a database is only as good as the last time it was worked. Map a contact two years ago and by now they have been promoted, moved on, or become the one nobody can reach. Keeping hundreds of those relationships warm costs time, and a model funded by volume does not pay for time spent on people who might not place for months.

There is a vetting problem underneath it too. [Generalist recruiters cannot accurately assess a mission-critical CV](https://www.connectingexperts.com/key-hires-defining-data-center-success-in-2026) (Connecting Experts), so on a no-fee model you are often sent the closest match already in the system, with a gentle nudge to lower the bar, rather than the right engineer found on purpose.

## What a bad hire actually costs

On a mission-critical project worth a hundred million euros and up, the recruitment fee was never the expensive line. The wrong hire is. A seat left empty for six months while the schedule slips is.

Set against a delayed handover or a commissioning failure, "free until placed" is a small saving balanced on top of the biggest risk on the job. The saving is visible and arrives upfront. The cost is larger and arrives later, which is exactly why the cheap option keeps getting chosen.

## What to look for in a data centre recruiter

Judge the model before the rate. What matters is whether the recruiter is actually paid to do the work a scarce senior hire needs: mapping the market now, approaching people who are not looking, and vetting them against the real demands of a mission-critical environment.

That work has to be funded to happen, which is what commitment upfront buys and a contingency fee does not. It also aligns both sides on one role done properly, instead of your vacancy competing for attention with twenty others on a busy desk. None of which means every role needs a retained search. The market's own read is the sensible one: use volume recruiters for operational positions, and specialists for the hard-to-fill roles that carry the build. The skill is knowing which is which, and being honest about it.

The one appointment you cannot afford to get wrong is the one the cheapest model is worst at making. That is the whole case.
